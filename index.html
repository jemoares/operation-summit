Velocity Summit 2026 Wrap-up

More than 120 operations, supply chain, fulfilment and last-mile leaders gathered for the first Operations Leaders’ Velocity Summit. Kieran Boyce shares what stuck with him, and why honest conversations about what went wrong are worth more than the highlight reel.

By Kieran Boyce

[Publish date]  •  6 min read

Hero image placeholder

What LinkedIn doesn’t show you

Scroll this platform on any given day and you’ll see all of our best days. Promotions, keynotes, panel wins, the photo with the good lighting. LinkedIn is our highlight reel. What you won’t see much of: the almighty screw-up, the delivery promise that failed a customer, the 2am anxiety-ridden replay in your head of the meeting that didn’t go to plan.

Operations is at the front-line of most businesses, that means they’re deep in the chaos ruthlessly prioritising in real time. The brutal fact is we don’t always get it right. You’d be hard pressed to find someone writing a long post, with all the beautiful emojis and tags about the day they got it wrong.

Authenticity and vulnerability in operations leadership come from exactly those moments, the ones we don’t post about. We call it experience once we’ve survived it; it’s what you get immediately after you needed it... That’s the premise the Velocity stream was built on over the past 12 months: build a forum where that experience gets shared honestly, instead of curated into another highlight reel. Don’t pull up the ladder behind us as leaders. Set the stage for the future leaders and operators by giving them something useful.

At Velocity 2026 that premise got a proper run. More than 120 retail operations, supply chain, fulfilment and last-mile leaders filled a room at The Grounds of Alexandria for the first Operations Leaders’ Velocity Summit, run by the NORA ~ National Online Retailers Association. Mick Bunt and I joined forces to host. It was the culmination of a year of warehouse tours, roundtables and conversations with the people keeping retail moving, including visits behind the scenes at Shiperoo, Winning Group and Culture Kings. Those tours shaped a lot of what made it into the room on the day.

Here’s what stuck with me.

Efficiency is dead, intelligence wins

I sat on a panel with Toni Baston Woolworths MarketPlus (Gee I miss working with you Toni, when Mick & I discussed the speaker list you were one of the first names that came up as authenticity personified!) and Shane Alexander (King Living), hosted by Mick (3PO), under the banner of “Efficiency is Dead. Intelligence Wins.”

Shane made a point that’s stayed with me. When you’re delivering high-end furniture, shaving two minutes off a delivery window is the wrong metric. His drivers need more time in the home, not less. Customer delight and margin protection in heavy goods come from intelligence applied to the last mile, not from stopwatch thinking. Experience matters.

I put a version of that same tension to the room from a experiential retail angle. Traditional supply chain thinking wants to standardise everything it touches. Standardisation of service eventually reaches a point where it strips out the touch customers are actually paying for (and expect). The job is finding that point and using data to protect it without blowing out cost.

The bigger point underneath all of it: digital and supply chain used to sit in separate silos. They don’t anymore, and any leader still running them that way is maintaining a obsolete structure.

Operations doesn’t stop at the warehouse door

Hakan Steele from Starshipit hosted a panel with three operators who’ve each scaled fast and have the scars to show for it: Joel Randal (Culture Kings), Jodie Tarasek (MESHKI) and Ty Cooper (Muscle Republic).

Ty’s point on headcount resonated most with anyone who’s operated a warehouse through a peak. Doubling your warehouse headcount doesn’t double your productivity. Without clear process, experienced leads and structured onboarding, more people just create more bottlenecks. Joel’s contribution was about where operational risk actually sits: not always inside the warehouse, but in the gap between marketing, ops, CX and tech when a campaign launches before all four have planned it together. Get that wrong and you’re looking at oversold inventory and a customer experience that takes weeks to recover - and potentially thousands of customers who will never shop with you again.

Jodie’s framing was great: Customers don’t separate your marketing, your warehouse, your customer service or your carriers. They remember whether the experience they were promised got delivered. Abi Bennett from Starshipit put it plainly in a note she sent after the session:

Operations is no longer simply supporting the brand experience. Increasingly, it is the brand experience.

Abi Bennett, Starshipit

She left the room asking two questions worth sitting with. (1) Could your next campaign overwhelm your operation? and; (2) Are you measuring what customers actually notice, rather than what your internal efficiency reports say?

The end of business as usual

Guy Nappa, COO of Oz Hair and Beauty, sat down with Mick for a fireside on what he called the end of business as usual. Guy’s business has been on a fierce growth curve for years. The conversation covered what breaks under that kind of growth: the role he used to do versus the role he does now, priorities that shift every six months whether you’re ready or not, and the mindset required to keep making decisive calls without waiting for things to settle.

The advice that stuck: navigating rapid growth means constantly deciding what to stop doing. There’s never going to be more time to find.

Diversity of decisions, not diversity of headcount

Mina Shi (LIONESS) closed out the afternoon on diversity in supply chain, and the framing was sharper than the topic usually gets. Their point: diversity of background and perspective is what produces better decisions, better teams and better businesses. That’s the measure worth tracking.

It’s a common thematic that operations leaders often tread their path unconventially. The “10-years-on-the-floor” is the exception pathway for modern leaders. This was it’s own useful reminder that the pipeline for operations talent is wider than the industry usually treats it, and diversity of thought can be unlocked by embracing it.

What I also took in this session was leadership from the back of the room. Brian Nguyen showing strong support. This is one of those unsung moments where a C-Suite executive take the time to support one of their team. It’s meaningful. People remember how you make them feel.

Where to next?

We built this stream because the tours, roundtables and now the summit kept surfacing the same thing: the operators willing to talk honestly about what went wrong learn faster than the ones only willing to talk about what went right. That’s an uncomfortable thing to build a public forum around, but it’s the version we believe is worth building.

The next generation of operations leaders is watching how the current one talks about failure. If all we show them is the highlight reel, we’re teaching them to hide the parts of the job that actually build judgement, experience and ultimately - value.

So here’s the question I’m sitting with after the day: if you stripped the highlight reel out of your own LinkedIn history, what would be left?
